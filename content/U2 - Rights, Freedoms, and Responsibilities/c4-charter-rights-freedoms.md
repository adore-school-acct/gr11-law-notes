# C4 - Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms

## Recognizing Rights and Freedoms

**right:** legal, moral, social entitlement that belongs to all people simply because they are human beings

**freedom:** the ability to conduct affairs as we see fit w/o being hampered or frustrated

### Historical Documents

*Magna Carta* (1215): first step in establishing basic rights for English people

*Bill of Rights* (1869) gave British parliament supremacy over monarchyu and extended certain civil and political rights such as...

- free elections
- reasonable bail and fines
- freedom from cruel or unusual punishment

**inalienable rights:** guaranteed entitlements that cannot be taken away because they are part of our nature

- Inalienable rights charts
  - *Declaration of Independence*, 1776, United States
  - *Declaration of the Rights of Man*, 1789, France
  - *Universal Declaration of Human Rights*, 1948, United Nations (UN)
    - includes fundamental freedoms of thought, opinion, expression, conscience, religion, and peaceful assembly
    - equal rights of men and women
    - equality before law
    - presumed innocent before proven guilty
    - education rights
    - freedom from torture or inhumane punishment

### Evolution of Canadian Rights

- **franchise:** right to vote
- Part of Canadian Human Rights history
  - Slavery
  - Limited franchise
  - Discriminatory laws and practices against Aboriginal peoples, non-whites, and immigrants
  - Anti-Asian riots
  - Women being classified as "non-persons"

*History of Human Rights in Canada*

- 1833: Slavery is abolished under *British Emancipation Act*
- 1884: *Indian Act* amended to outlaw cultural / religious cermonies like potlach
- 1900: *Chinese Immigration Act* increases $50 head tax to $100
- 1900: *Dominion Exclusions Act* excludes minorities from federal elections
- 1908: "No stoppage" rule requires immigrants to travel directly to Canada &rarr; reduces Indian immigrants
- 1914: *Komagata Maru* incident &mdash; 24 / 376 potential Indian immigrants allowed to enter Canada
- 1916: Manitoba grants women franchise in prov. elections
- 1918: Women granted franchise in fed. elections
- 1920: Women allowed to run for fed. office
- 1920: Fed. gov't. makes franchise universal, excluding some minorities and Status Indians
- 1921: Qc. court upholds right of theatre owner to refuse a black patron to sit in orchestra seats
- 1927: *Indian Act* amended to prohibit Aboriginal peoples to hire lawyers for land claims w/o consent of the superintendent general of Indian Affairs
- 1928: Alberta passes *Sexual Sterilization Act*, orders sterilization of patients in psychiatric hospitals
- 1928: S.C.C. decides that women are not "persons"
- 1929: "Famous Five" &mdash; Emily Murphy, Nellie McClung, Louise McKinney, Henrietta Muir Edwards, and Irene Parlby appeals "person decision" to Privy Council of England, which rules that women are "persons"
- 1934: People libelled bcz. of race or creed can get court order to stop the libel
- 1940: Communist Party outlawed under *War Measures Act*
- 1942: Hutterites, Doukhobors, and enemy aliens barred from buying land
- 1942: Japanese Canadians forcibly removed from their homes and sent to detention camps
- 1945: Covenant restraining sale of land to Jews is struck down
- 1947: *Saskatchewan Bill of Rights Act* is first broad human rights statute passed in Canada
- 1948: People of all races excl. Status Indians given fed. franchise
- 1952: S.C.C. rules that Jehovah's Witnesses can distribute religious pamphlets
- 1953: *Fair Employment Practices Act*
- 1956: *Female Employeees Equal Pay Act*
- 1960: Status Indians granted fed. franchise
- 1960: *Canadian Bill of Rights*

### *Canadian Bill of Rights*

***Canadian Bill of Rights:*** First attempt in protecting Canadian human rights enacted in Parliament by PM John G. Diefenbaker, which recognized:

- rights of individuals to life, liberty, security of the person, and enjoyment of property
- freedom of religion, speech, assembly, and association
- freedom of the press
- right to counsel and right to a fair hearing

*Limitations*

- Applied to matters only under federal jurisdiction
- Diefenbaker intended it to apply to provinces, but it did not
- *Bill of Rights* had same force as other statutes and a judge could overrule it
- Could be amended by a majority vote in House of Commons, changing or eliminating protections

### Entrenching Rights and Freedoms

*Bill of Rights* didn't offer Canadians enough protections and Pierre Trudeau wanted to **entrech** it into the *Constitution*

**entrench:** include or incorporate one thing into another thing

Some provinces feared a shift of power, so a compromise was made to allow them to **override** around the *Charter*

**overide:** to prevail over

## Jurisdiction and Enforcement

### Jurisdiction

*Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms* applies to:

- all branches and levels of government
- Crown corporations
- federally incorporated companies
- banks
- other federally regulated organization

Does not apply to situations that DO NOT involve the gov't. (i.e. unfair treatment by landlord)

### Enforcement

S. 24 (1) of the *Charter* gives people right to challenge gov't. if they believe their *Charter* rights have been violated by gov't. or its agencies

- *Charter* written in general language
- Role of judges and tribunals expanded so that they can interpret and enforce its terms
- They must determine whether a law violates the *Charter*, and what remedy should be applied if it does

**Questions to determine violation of the *Charter***

1. Was the right infringed or violated by government or its agencies?
2. Is the right in question covered under the *Charter*?
   1. some rights covered in labour acts and human rights codes rather than *Charter* 
3. Is the violation or infringement withing a reasonable limit?
   1. must decide if violation falls under s. 1

Offending legislation is struck down using s. 52 of the *Constitution Act*

Certain length of time given to amend or repeal that legislation

### Limitations to Rights and Freedoms

#### Section 1: Reasonable Limits Clause

- Rights are not absolute
- Rights and freedoms are guaranteed within "reasonable limits"
- "Reasonable limits" are determined based on verdict of *R. v. Oakes*, [1986] 1 S.C.R. 103
  - During trial in 1982, David Oakes convicted of illegal possession of narcotics
  - Oakes argued that s. 8 of *Narcotic Control Act* (placed burden of proof on accused), violated presumption of innocence in s. 11(d) of *Charter*
  - Appeal court ruled that "reasonable limits" from s. 1 were justified

*Reasonable Limits criteria / Oakes test*

1. Reason for limiting a *Charter* right must be shown to be important enough to justify overriding a Constitutional right
2. There must be rational connection between limitation of rights and objective of legislation
3. Right must be limited as little as possible
4. More severe the rights limitation &rarr; more important the objective must be

#### Section 33: Notwithstanding Clause

**notwithstanding clause:** s. 33 of the *Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms* that allows fed. and prov. gov'ts. to pass legislation exempt from s. 2 and ss. 7-15 of the *Charter*

- Compromise which allows gov't. to override some rights/freedoms in *Charter* if necessary
  - i.e. prison violated a person's right to security of person
- Legislation overriding *Charter* via s. 33 must be reviewed every 5 years to prevent abuse
- At the end of 5 years, gov't. may renew its intent to **invoke** s. 33 and override *Charter* right
- **invoke:** to put into effect

Gov'ts. reluctant to invoke s. 33 but it has been used

- Qc. gov't. invoked s. 33 through Bill 178 when it passed a law permitting signs in French only, violated s. 2(b)
- 1928-1972: Alb. gov't. permitted sterilization of people deemed mentally disabled
  - 1996: Leilani Munir, victim, successfully sued gov't. and awarded monetary compensation
  - Then-premier Ralph Klein introduced bill that limits compensation to $150k / claim and protect gov't. from legal challenges using s. 33
  - Public outcry caused withdrawal of proposed bill

## Fundamental Freedoms (s. 2)

### Section 2(a): Freedom of Conscience and Religion

Right to:

- believe in anything you choose
- declare your beliefs openly w/o fear
- express your religous beliefs through practice, worhsip, teaching, and dissemination
- **dissemination:** spreading ideas widely

You may **NOT be forced** to act in a way against your beliefs / conscience

- i.e., same-sex marriage is legal, but member of clergy is not obliged to conduct this marriage if it is against his/her beliefs

*Examples*

- *R.* v. *Big M Drug Mart Ltd.*, [1985] 1 S.C.R. 295 &mdash; tested *Lord's Day Act*, prohibiting sale of certain goods on Sunday, struck down as contrary to s. 2(a)
  - It "forced" people to believe Sunday was a day of rest
- Upholds employee's rights for accomodations for religious holidays
- "Reasonable limits:" Blood transfustions are against a parent's religion, but court orders it for the child because his/her survival comes first

### Section 2(b): Freedom of Thought and Expression

You are free to:

- think and believe what you want
- publicly express your opinions through:
  - writing
  - speech
  - painting
  - photography
  - other means
- 2(b) also includes freedom of the press

Key democratic freedom that courts don't want to restrict unless absolutely necessary

*Examples*

- *International Fund for Animal Welfare Inc.* v. *The Queen*, [1989] 1 F.C. 335
  - Court ruled that not allowing members of press from approaching a commercial seal hunt violated freedom of the press to gather info.
- *Irwin Toy Ltd.* v. *Québec* [1989] 1 S.C.R. 927
  - Issue: limitation placed on ads directed to children <13 yrs. by the *Consumer Protection Act* of Qc.
  - Court ruling: *Act* did not infringe unreasonably on Irwin's freedom of expression
- *R.* v. *Keegstra*, [1990] 3 S.C.R. 697
  - Mr. Keegstra, high school teacher, expressed extremely negative views towards Jewish people
  - Denied that 6 mil. people died in Holocaust and blamed Jews for world's problems
  - Court ruling: *Criminal Code* prohibition against spreading hatred is too broad but his 2(b) freedom restrictions were in "reasonable limits", s. 1

### Section 2(c) and 2(d): Freedom of Peaceful Assembly and Association

#### Section 2(c): Freedom of Peaceful Assembly

Canadians have the right to assemble for peaceful purposes, i.e.

- demonstrating against a gov't.
- march in support of a cause
- assembly must be "peaceful"
  - riot is not "peaceful"
  - riot must consist of 3+ persons prepared to use force to execute common purpose, in a manner alarming to a reasonable person

#### Section 2(d): Freedom of Association

Right / freedom to connect w/ other people or groups such as:

- unions
- political parties
- cultural groups
- educational organizations
- sports clubs

*Limitations*

- Young offenders may be ordered to not associate w/ some of their friends in probation
- Prison inmate can't associate w/ everyone otherwise discipline and security will be broken
- Some groups like RCMP officers or fed. lawyers cannot form or join unions

## Democratic and Mobility Rights

### Sections 3-5: Democratic Rights

#### Section 3: The Right to Vote

Every "citizen" has the right to vote in an election and run for office w/ restrictions like:

- age
- residence
- registration
- restrictions on certain groups
  - i.e. all inmates were prohibited to vote, but are now permitted

Guarantees voting rights and rights to exercise them regularly

#### Section 4: Elections Every 5 Years

- Canadians assured opportunity to vote every 5 years except...
- ...under extraordinary situations like:
  - war
  - national emergency

#### Section 5: Sessions in Parliament and Legislative Assemblies

Parliament and legislative assemblies must hold at least one session a year.

Guarantee provides opportunity for elected members and public to question gov't. policy

### Section 6: Mobility Rights

#### Section 6 (1): Right to remain in, enter, or leave Canada

All Canadians have the right to remain in, enter, or leave Canada

Most cases in s. 6 (1) involve **extradition**

**extradition:** surrendering an accused person to another jurisdiction to face trail there

The Canadian gov't. can extradite a person to face trail at another country but will not do so if they will face a **capital offence**, since it violates the right to life

**capital offence:** crime punishable by death

#### Section 6 (2): Right to Move and Pursue Livelihood in Any Province

All Canadian citizens or permanent residents have the right to

- a) move to and take residence in any province
- b) pursue gaining livelihood in any province

#### Section 6 (3): Restrictions to Protect Existing Provincial Residents

- Gov'ts. of poorer provinces concered that outsiders will take away their already scarce jobs
- Publicly funded social services can be restricted to those who have lived in province for certain amount of time

#### Section 6 (4): Right to Create Programs to Favour Own Permanent Residents

Province w/ higher unemployment rate than rest of Canada can create programs to favour its own permanent residents.

## Legal and Equality Rights

### Legal Rights

#### Section 7: Life, Liberty, and Security of the Person

Everyone has the right to life, liberty, and security of the person

Cannot be deprived of these rights except in accordance w/ principles of fundamental justice

*Difficult s. 7 cases*

- Abortion: The right to life for an unborn person
  - S.C.C. Ruling: A fetus is not a *person*, so it has no right to life
- *Rodriguez* v. *British Columbia (A.G.)*, [1993] 3 S.C.R. 519
  - **Topic:** A terminally ill person's right to assisted suicide
  - Case: Sue Rodriguez suffered from ALS, a disease that attacks central nervous system
  - She wanted to end her life at a time of her choosing but knew she would be incapable of killing herself when the time came
  - s. 241(b) of the *Criminal Code* prevents anyone from assisting her sucide
  - Rodriguez argued that s. 241(b) violated her s. 7 rights
  - S.C.C. Ruling: 5-to-4 vote that assisted suicide should not be legalized &mdash; purpose of s. 241(b) is to protect life
  
#### Section 8: Unreasonable Search and Seizure

People cannot be subject to unreasonable search and seizure.

Police must have good reason for searching the person, home, or belongings of the accused.

- Search of person must be done fairly
  - Police CAN search place of arrest for evidence relating to offence
  - They CANNOT do "fishing expeditions," find something else that can be used against the suspect in court

#### Section 9: Arbitrary Detention or Imprisonment

"[E]veryone has the right not be arbitarily detained or imprisoned."

People can't be held for questioning, arrested, or jailed by police w/o good reason

*Example*

- Question: Are police roadside checks illegal?
- Answer: *R. v. Ladouceur*, [1990] 1 S.C.R. 1257
  - Gerald Ladouceur was stopped by police during a traffic check
  - Discovered that his driver's license was suspended
  - Charged of driving while his license was under suspension
  - Ladouceur argued that traffic checks violated ss. 7, 8, and 9 of the *Charter*
  - S.C.C. Ruling: Police stops do violate s. 9 but they are justified under s. 1 (reasonable limits)

#### Section 10: Rights When Arrested

When someone is arrested or detained, they have these rights:

1. be informed promptly of the reason for arrest or detention
   1. *promptly* means reason given during arrest not after, according to judges
   2. if accused believes arrest is unlawful, he can apply to have validity of arrest determined via *habeus corpus* (C1)
2. be informed of right to obtain legal counsel
   1. does not mean right to obtain lawyer
   2. means accused has right to seek advice of counsel
3. If accused can't afford legal counsel, they must be advised that legal aid is available
   1. not everyone qualifies for legal aid &rarr; have job, no legal aid (gen. rule)
   2. lawyer (duty counsel) will appear in court to offer initial legal counsel for those who can't afford a lawyer

#### Section 11: Rights When Charged

s. 11(a): Accused must be informed of the specific offence w/o unreasonable delay

s. 11(b): Accused must be tried within reasonable amount of time

- *R. v. Askov*, [1990] 2 S.C.R. 1199 &mdash; Askov waited almost 3 years for trial and Court ruled that violated 11(b)
  - Charges against Askov were dismissed

---

- s. 11(c): accused cannot be forced to testify at his/her own trial
- s. 11(d): presumption of innocence until proven guilty
- s. 11(e): accused cannot be denied reasonable bail w/o just cause
- s. 11(f): individuals have right to jury trial except in military offences unless the punishment exceeds 5 years imprisonment or more severe punishment
- s. 11(g): accused cannot be convicted unless law under which he/she was charged was in force at the time
  - specifically states that actions in question were illegal
- s. 11(h): if person found guilty and is punished for the offence, he/she cannot be tried or punished for it again &mdash; "double jeopardy"
- s. 11(i): if someone commits offence and before sentencing, a new law alters fine or term of imprisonment ...
  - person must be sentenced under the more *lenient* law

#### Section 12: Cruel and Unusual Treatment or Punishment

Governments may not treat or punish individuals in an unnecessarily harsh fashion.

"Cruel and unusual treatment or punishment" still has no clear definition

*Examples*

- *R. v. Smith (Edward Dewey)*, [1987] 1 S.C.R. 1045
  - case involving import of 213 g cocaine
  - S.C.C. explained that in order to determine whether punishment was too harsh, it must consider the:
    - gravity of the offence
    - personal characteristics of the offender
    - particular circumstances of the case
  - Court assesses the punishment using the "most innocent possible" test
  - Justice Lamer used ex. of whether young person entering Canada w/ 1 weed cig. should go to jail for 7 yrs.
    - min. sentence in s. 5 (2) of *Narcotic Control Act* R.S.C. 1970
    - s. 5 (2) was contrary to ss. 7, 9, 12 of *Charter*
- Dangerous offender provision of *Criminal Code* allows criminals who have committed certain # of offences to be declared dangerous and kept in jail *indefinitely*
  - Unsuccessfully challenged under s. 12 in
    - *R. v. Lyons*, [1987] 2 S.C.R. 309
    - *R. v. Milne*, [1987] 2 S.C.R. 512
  - *Steele v. Mountain Institution*, [1990] 2 S.C.R. 1385
    - Court ruled that denying a "dangerous offender" parole violated s. 12

#### Sections 13-14: Rights of Witnesses in Court

**Section 13**

Witnesses giving evidence in court cannot have their testimony used against them.

*Example:* Bob is giving testimony about a hit-and-run accident because he was involved in drug deal there.

Bob's admission of selling drugs can't be used against him if he is tried on drug-related charges.

**Section 14**

Anyone in court who is hearing impaired or cannot understand / speak the language in court has the right to an interpreter.

### Section 15: Equality Rights (plus s. 27-28)

#### Section 15 (1): Equality Section

"[E]very individual is equal before and under the law and has the right to equal protection and equal benefit of the law without discrimination...."

#### Section 15 (2): Equity Section

Programs set up by gov'ts to **ameliorate** the conditions of certain disavantaged groups / individuals are permissible even if they seem discriminatory

**ameliorate:** to improve

s. 15 begins w/ "every individual" which excludes corporations

S.C.C. defined discrimination in *Andrews v. Law Society of British Columbia*, [1989]

## Language and General Rights

### Sections 16-22: Language Rights

- English or French can be used in Parliament
- statutes, records, and journals of Parliament are to be published in both langs.
- members of public are entitled to communicate w/ fed. gov't. in either official lang.
- either language can be used in courts established by Parliament, incl. the S.C.C.

#### Language Education Rights

In English-speaking provinces, citizens have right to have children educated in French if:

- their 1st lang. is French
- they received their own primary education in Canada in French
- they have a child who is already receiving / received education in French

In Québec, citizens have right to have children educated in English if:

- they received their own primary education in Canada in English
- they have child who is receiving / received education in English

Sufficient number of children needed to justify schooling in either official language

### General Rights

#### Section 24 (1): Challenge Gov't.

Gives people right to challenge the government if their rights are being violated.

#### Section 25: Aboriginal Rights

- Intent: Protect culture, customs, traditions, languages, rights, and freedoms of Aboriginal peoples
- Rights and freedoms include those recognized by the Royal Proclamation of 1763 and...
- ...those existing / acquired through land-claim agreements
- Terms must not be seen as a way to **abrogate** or **derogate** the rights of Aboriginal peoples
- **abrogate:** to abolish or nullify
- **derogate:** to take away or detract
- Confers no new rights but protects Aboriginal rights from *Charter*

#### Sections 27 and 28: Multicultural Heritage and Gender Equality

- s. 27: Canada is multicultural and all rights in *Charter* must be interpreted in light of preservation and enhancement of multiculturalism
- s. 28: All rights and freedoms apply equally to men and women

### The Supreme Court and the *Charter*

- The *Constitution Act, 1982* gave more power to courts and the S.C.C.
- Supreme Court overturned laws passed by parliaments
- S.C.C. forced gov'ts. to amend laws to comply w/ rulings
- Some argue that s. 33 balances power between the S.C.C. and the parliament
- Others argue that s. 33 has been used many times to attempt to weaken the *Charter* rights
  - 1987: Sask. gov. used s. 33 to end strikes by public emplyees and impose a legal contract
  - Argument: Strikes disrupted services to Sask. residents
